## Part 1 — The job an OST actually does

One second, three questions, resolved **simultaneously**:

1. **"Is this about me?"** (identification)
2. **"What do I get?"** (dream outcome)
3. **"What does it cost me to find out?"** (risk, effort, time)

Nearly every failure in this repo passed 1 and skipped 2 and 3. "2.9kg"
answers "is this about me" and promises nothing.

What is *not* on the list: "is this interesting?" Interesting is what a
fact is when it has no owner and no payoff. The system has been
optimising for interesting.

## Part 2 — The niche vocabulary iceberg

**This is the highest-leverage idea in the entire research folder and
it was barely used.** From `research/briar-cochran-notes-accordion-tam-ccn.txt`,
Briar's own words, paraphrased tightly:

> Every niche has top-of-iceberg words. Gardening: seeds, planting,
> tilling. Every gardener thinks about these, and nothing profound
> lives there. The real gardeners are talking about the metric that sun
> rays give off. Ask: **if 20 experts in this niche were in a meeting
> together, what would they actually be talking about?** They are not
> talking about the basics. Then move that keyword, that buzzword, into
> your on-screen hook.

His example title: **"9 ways to save 15% on my hydroponics setup in my
basement."** And his own counter-positioned OST, "hooks and edits don't
matter," works because it speaks to what high-level creators actually
argue about, not what beginners search.

### What this corrects

I have now given Luke two *different wrong* answers about the keyword.

- First wrong answer: coin an insider term. That produced "Cadence
  Lock," which was invented and unsourced (P39).
- Second wrong answer, in the previous version of this file: use plain
  language for something the viewer already wants. That is only true
  for broad-TAM topics.

**The actual rule:** the keyword should be **real vocabulary from deep
in the ICP's iceberg, that they already use and already worry about.**
Not invented, not top-of-iceberg generic. "Junk miles" is genuinely
iceberg-deep running vocabulary and was a defensible keyword; what
killed that OST was the missing outcome, not the word. "Morning
mobility routine" is shallow vocabulary, and it works because that
video's TAM is everyone over 30, not a specialist audience.

**Depth of vocabulary should match the TAM you're aiming at (P1).**
High-TAM video, shallow familiar words. Core-audience video, iceberg
words. Getting this backwards is what makes content feel either
generic or impenetrable.

### Running's iceberg, drawn from the real Reddit research

- **Surface (avoid as the keyword):** running, easy runs, getting
  faster, marathon training, staying motivated.
- **Mid:** long run, taper, carb load, zone 2, PB, DNF.
- **Deep, what the 20 experts actually argue about, all real and
  present in `research/reddit-running-research-manual.txt`:** junk
  miles vs base building, threshold and LTHR2, cardiac drift and
  decoupling, run/walk ratios, 80/20 polarised training, step cycles,
  glycogen depletion and the wall, carb grams per hour, negative
  splitting, easy-pace creep, sodium and cramp, shakeout runs,
  supercompensation.

That list is the raw material for keywords. It came out of Luke's own
research file, which means it clears P39 by construction.

## Part 3 — Consequence of the pain, not the pain

From `research/heaton-ralston-personal-brand.txt`, and it is the single
most actionable thing in that document:

> Stop writing hooks that sound like ads. Don't call out the person.
> **Don't even call out the pain. Call out the consequence of the
> pain.** "Are you tired of being bald?" is not interesting. **"Are you
> tired of wearing a hat all the time?"** definitely piques interest.

This is the exact mechanism missing from every OST I have produced.
Compare:

| Pain (what I wrote) | Consequence of the pain (what works) |
|---|---|
| "Junk Miles Test" | "Your Sunday long run is stealing Monday" |
| "Shin Splints Fix" | "The 3 weeks you lose every time you ramp up" |
| "Mile-1 Panic" | "Planning your race around where the toilets are" |
| "Runner's Imposter Syndrome" | "Waiting until the car park is empty to start your watch" |

The right column is behaviour the viewer recognises in themselves, and
recognition is involuntary. The left column is a category label they
have to care about first.

### The rest of that document, also under-used

- **Idea = their painful problem + your unique solution.** Not topic
  first.
- **Write 10 things your niche says that you disagree with, then hammer
  them.** A concrete, generative counter-positioning exercise (P2)
  that this system has never actually run for Luke.
- **World-building:** named concepts, shared language, characters,
  settings, weaknesses, legends and lore, and most importantly **a
  common enemy** to rally around. Luke has no named common enemy yet.
  Candidates from his own voice: pace snobbery, watch worship, the
  "just run more" crowd.
- **Promise, progress, payoff.** Breaking a promise made in the hook
  breaks trust permanently. This is the script-level companion to the
  OST.
- **You don't determine quality, the audience does.**

## Part 4 — Teardown: the three examples Luke named

### "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" (New Balance)

Luke quoted it as "models"; the commonly cited version is
"supermodels," which widens the mechanism. Verify before repeating it
publicly (P39).

Structure: `worn by + [Pole A: max status, max distance] + and + [Pole
B: min status, max familiarity]`

1. **It is a span, not a list.** Two poles, and the reader auto-fills
   everything between. A list of five customer types would feel
   *smaller*, because a list is finite and a span is infinite.
2. **It resolves the category's two-sided anxiety in one move.** "Will
   I look like I'm trying too hard" (dads in Ohio) and "is this
   actually still cool" (supermodels in London). Most lines answer one.
3. **"Ohio" does work no adjective could.** Not "America," not "the
   Midwest." A proper noun carries a compressed identity. Swap in "dads
   in New York" and the line dies, because **the message lives in the
   gap between the two nouns, not in the nouns themselves.**
4. **Forced self-location.** You cannot read it without silently
   answering "which am I?" That is involuntary engagement.
5. **The benefit is never stated.** It states *who*, and lets you
   compute *everyone*. A derived conclusion is held harder than a
   stated one.
6. **It sounds pre-existing.** Two-beat parallel, matched syllable
   weight, L-alliteration in "London." Proverb rhythm borrows authority
   it has not earned.

### "4 elite conversation starters you can use anytime"

`[small countable number] + [status class] + [portable tool] +
[deployability clause]`

- **"4"** caps the time cost, signals curation (what survived the cut),
  and sits under the note-taking threshold. Round numbers read padded.
- **"elite"** is not a quality word, it is a **membership** word. It
  imports the authority a credential would otherwise have to supply.
- **"conversation starters"** is a *tool*, countable and possessable.
  "How to be better at socialising" is a *topic*: unpossessable,
  unmemorable. **Tools beat topics.**
- **"you can use anytime"** kills the real objection ("this only works
  if you're already confident / at parties / not in my industry") in
  three words, and converts knowledge into deployment.
- **No curiosity gap at all.** Worth sitting with: curiosity is what
  you use when the value is not self-evident. It is not mandatory.

### "morning mobility routine that makes me feel 25 at 35"

`[time-anchored named practice] + that makes me + [felt state as a
two-number delta]`

- **"morning"** kills "when would I even do this" and implies brevity.
- **"mobility routine"** is a possessable practice, shallow vocabulary
  matched to a broad TAM (Part 2).
- **"makes me feel"** is testimony about a **felt state**:
  unfalsifiable, therefore unarguable, and exactly what the viewer
  wants. "Will make you faster" invites argument.
- **"25 at 35"** is the engine. Two numbers ten years apart in four
  syllables. The viewer performs the subtraction involuntarily. It
  converts an abstract desire into something that reads as evidence.
  It self-targets (a 35-year-old sees themselves exactly). And the
  digits reverse, so it scans like a formula and sticks.
- **The creator is the proof, not the hero** (P30). "Makes *me* feel"
  offers: do what I did, get what I got.

### Losers

| OST | Views | Tail names |
|---|---|---|
| "Q: How to know if you 'actually' raced? (When most take part)" | 133 | abstract judgement |
| "How to run when you 'think' everyone is watching (*Spotlight effect)" | 313 | **the mechanism's jargon** |
| "One missed session out of 1,200 hours (but you lie awake like it's all over)" | 462 | **a feeling** |
| "The 'real' problem with running everything easy (+what I do now)" | 518 | **the creator's behaviour** |

| "How to log off without guilt" | 679 | gain, but no specificity |

### The rule

> **The tail names what the VIEWER walks away with. Never the
> mechanism, the method's name, a feeling, or the creator.**

"1 Lamppost At A Time" is concrete, specific, real and charming, and it
did 561. **Concreteness was never the variable. Ownership of the payoff
is.**

### Three more findings from the same data

- **"How to" beats "How I":** 2,266 vs 561, same account, same
  audience.
- **Honest counter-example:** "When you 'actually' need a gel (*To
  absolutely smash your race)" has a gain tail and did 631. Best
  reading: hype phrase instead of a specific outcome, plus a narrow
  topic. Held as a caveat, not explained away (P36).
- **The two biggest non-how-to winners do a different job.** "Are they
  trail running or just walking really fast?" (2,578) is
  self-recognition, an in-group thought the viewer has had and never
  said. "What's it actually like to Climb Benbulben" (148,000) is
  vicarious experience. Neither promises a lesson because the feeling
  *is* the payoff. Do not judge these by the tail rule.

## Part 6 — ERN's account rewards a different shape entirely

This has never been analysed and it changes how the company account
should be written. Real data from `brands/engine-room-nutrition/voice.md`:

| ERN OST | Views | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| "Nobody expected us 2 to be next" | 2,158 | underdog narrative |
| "the bar that worked in a food lab was about to meet 1,200 metres of climbing" | 1,725 | **stakes setup, will it survive** |
| "POV: Climb Mount Brandon. Only survived bcause of the ERN bar" | 1,271 | POV + product proof |
| "Why 2 accountants made a Carb Bar" | 680 | origin story |
| "Welcome to the Rice Crisp Graveyard - Ep. 4" | 653 | series, world-building |
| "What to actually eat before a race (+ be your own hero)" | 645 | how-to |
| "Can the ERN Carb Bar Get us a 5KM PB?" | 598 | test question |
| "This is the morning that produced my fastest ever 5k. It shouldn't have." | 567 | narrative + contradiction |
| "You feel SO SLOW during races (How to fix it)" | 469 | how-to |
| "How to break your 4.8KM PB" | 462 | how-to |
| "How to run the last 10k faster than the first 10k" | 439 | how-to |

**Every how-to on ERN sits between 439 and 645. Every narrative-with-stakes
sits between 1,271 and 2,158.** That is the exact inverse of the
personal account, where how-to-with-gain dominates.

Read together, the two datasets say something coherent: **the personal
brand is followed for what the viewer can learn; the company account is
followed for what it can prove.** A product brand pre-launch has no
teaching authority yet, so instruction underperforms and jeopardy
overperforms. "The bar that worked in a food lab was about to meet
1,200 metres of climbing" is a *test with a possible failure*, and the
possibility of failure is the entire hook.

**Practical: stop writing how-to OSTs for ERN.** Write tests, trials,
jeopardy, and underdog narrative. Save how-to for the personal account.

### The P35 tension, stated honestly

"You're Not Unfit. You're Underfuelled" (3.4x) is tailing negation, the
shape P35 bans. It works here, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest. The distinction that holds: the banned version negates
toward a vague abstraction ("it's not what you think"). This version
**negates the viewer's self-diagnosis and replaces it with a specific,
iceberg-vocabulary alternative that changes what they do tomorrow.**
That is a diagnosis reversal, not a rhetorical tic. Still handle with
care, still never use it as the default, and never use it without a
specific second half.

- **Series numbering compounds hard.** Ross Mackay's "Building The
  Brand Ep. 55-64" occupies 9 of the top 25 slots, on a channel with a

- **"The Truth About X"** appears twice in his top 10 (12.4x, 11.4x).
- **Named person as the subject** carries: "Sam Smith Loves Hooters
  Chicken Wings" (38.4x), "Sabastian Sawe's..." (10.7x), "Sam Altman
  on..." (8.8x), "DHH: How to..." (4.3x). Borrowed identity is the
  single most reliable multiplier in this set, which is also exactly
  what P40 restricts, so it has to be earned through a real vehicle
  (interview, analysis, explicit narration), never lifted.
- **"Actually" and "REAL" as authenticity markers:** "What a $1B CEO
  Actually Does in a Day" (16.6x), "The REAL reason the US can't beat
  Iran" (10.0x), "How Money Actually Works" (3.9x), "The Only Strategy
  That Actually Works Long Term" (9.3x). Luke already uses "actually"
  natively (`voice.md`). This is confirmed across a second dataset.
- **Weird imperative:** "Cook Peppers Like You Hate Them" (4.0x). An
  instruction that shouldn't make sense creates a hard stop.
- **Exhaustiveness:** "I Made EVERY Salsa" (6.0x). Vicarious
  completeness, the Babish mechanism from P17's clarification note.

## Part 8 — The advertising canon, and the one idea that explains everything

These are well-documented in advertising history. Treat the mechanisms
as reliable; verify exact wording, dates and figures before repeating
any of them on camera (P39).

### Ogilvy

Already in P15: roughly five times as many people read the headline as
the body copy. His most famous headline is the working demonstration of
everything above:

> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes
> from the electric clock."

It never claims quietness. It **demonstrates** it by naming the loudest
surviving sound. Specific speed, specific component, zero adjectives.
The reader concludes "silent" themselves, which is the same derived-
conclusion mechanism as the New Balance span.

### John Caples

> "They Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano But When I Started to
> Play!—"

A complete story arc in a headline: social humiliation, then
vindication, with the payoff withheld at the dash. The reader casts
themselves as the protagonist. Caples' documented position, which is
the whole justification for this file: **headline changes alone can
move response by multiples, with identical body copy underneath.** His
four levers: self-interest, news, curiosity, and a quick easy way.

### Eugene Schwartz, and why running content feels like a sea of sameness

Two concepts from *Breakthrough Advertising*, and the second one
explains Luke's core complaint better than anything else in this repo.

**1. Awareness stages.** The reader is Unaware, Problem-Aware,
Solution-Aware, Product-Aware, or Most Aware, and **the headline must
meet them where they are.** Luke's ICP is mostly Problem-Aware (they
know they blow up at 30k, they know their easy runs feel hard) and
occasionally Solution-Aware. Writing "The Junk Miles Test" addresses a
Solution-Aware reader who already accepts the category. Writing "your
Sunday long run is stealing Monday" meets a Problem-Aware reader in
their actual experience.

**2. Market sophistication**, five stages. As a market matures:
simple claim, then bigger claim, then **mechanism**, then elaborated
mechanism, then **identification**. Running content is a saturated,
highly sophisticated market. That is *why* "there are 2 ways to get
faster" reads as meh and why every "how to run easy" video drowns.

> **A stage-4/5 market does not respond to claims. It responds to
> mechanism and identity.** This is the same conclusion Briar reached
> from the other direction with the iceberg (Part 2) and Kallaway
> reached with originality density (P31). Three independent sources,
> one answer.

Practically, for Luke: lead with the *mechanism* (why the thing happens
physiologically, named precisely) or with *identity* (who this is for,
who they are becoming), and stop leading with claims about outcomes
everyone else is also claiming.

### Claude Hopkins

*Scientific Advertising*: specifics multiply the weight of an argument.
His documented Schlitz case is directly relevant to ERN: he described
the ordinary, unglamorous brewing process that every brewer used and
nobody bothered to explain, and the brand moved from fifth to first.

> **The process that is boring to you is fascinating to the audience,
> because they have never seen it.**

That is exactly why "the bar that worked in a food lab was about to
meet 1,200 metres of climbing" (1,725) beat every ERN how-to, and why
the Rice Crisp Graveyard series has legs.

### Gary Halbert

His documented answer, when asked what advantage he'd want in selling
burgers: **a starving crowd.** Market beats copy. Which is Briar's
"topic beats hook" (P16) arriving from advertising a generation
earlier, and it is why Part 2 sits above every other section here.

## Part 9 — Movie titles: sayability and visualisability

The user asked specifically about titles "like a movie," and the craft
transfers in three ways.

**1. Compression to an image.** *Jaws.* *Alien.* *Heat.* One word that
produces a picture and a feeling before any plot is known. The test
that matters for OSTs: **can the viewer see it?** "The Junk Miles Test"
produces no image. "Your Sunday long run is stealing Monday" produces a
Monday.

**2. Premise fully loaded into the title.** *Snakes on a Plane.* *12
Angry Men.* *Dead Poets Society.* The title is the pitch; no additional
setup is needed. The OST equivalent is the ERN winner: "the bar that
worked in a food lab was about to meet 1,200 metres of climbing." That
is *Snakes on a Plane* for a carb bar. It is also, not coincidentally,
ERN's second-best-performing post.

**3. Sayability.** Titles that survive are the ones people can repeat
to a friend without effort. Short, concrete, rhythmically closed.
"Junk miles" passes. "The Recovery Run Rule (pick right, every time, in
5 seconds)" does not, because nobody says that out loud.

**The three-part title test, borrowed from film and applied here:**
- **Sayable:** could someone repeat it after hearing it once?
- **Visualisable:** does it produce a picture?
- **Ownable:** if it works, does it become yours (a recurring name,
  a series, a phrase your audience adopts)?

Ownability is where world-building (P11, Heaton's "named concepts and
shared language") meets the numbers in Part 7: Ross Mackay's episodic
series holding 9 of the top 25 slots is ownability compounding.

## Part 10 — The visual layer

Luke asked about visualisation, and half of this is already documented
in Briar's own masterclass transcript. Consolidated with what the
outlier data shows.

### Typography and layout, from Briar's transcript verbatim in substance

- **Fonts:** native platform font, or one deliberate luxury font with
  real typography thought behind it. Nothing else.
- **No function words on screen.** Cut "and," "so," "the," "is." Every
  word should be a keyword, proper noun, or verb. Adjectives are mostly
  waste.
- **Two lines maximum.** Three overwhelms.
- **Break lines by structure, not by where the text box runs out.**
  This is a real and commonly missed detail.
- **Hold it for 10 seconds, possibly the whole video.** Not the native
  2-3 second cut rhythm. The eye has to read the text, drop to the
  visual, and come back up.
- **Placement:** bold headline box top-of-frame, smaller subtext lower,
  per the real screenshots in HF10.

### What the image itself has to do

- **The frame and the text must complete each other, not repeat each
  other.** If the text says "1,200 metres of climbing" the frame should
  show the mountain, not the words again.
- **One focal point.** The Ogilvy-style post Luke admired (HF12) works
  because the pairing *is* the focal point: two faces, an arrow, proof
  artefacts, a series banner.
- **Jeopardy is visible; instruction is not.** Part 6's finding has a
  visual corollary: a test with a possible failure gives you something
  to *show* (the climb, the lab, the prototype). A how-to gives you a
  talking head. That is part of why ERN's narrative posts beat its
  how-tos.
- **A face with an unresolved expression outperforms a neutral one.**
  Standard thumbnail practice, consistent with the self-recognition and
  jeopardy mechanisms above.

## Part 11 — The mechanism library

Named so they can be chosen deliberately.

**Identity and targeting**
1. **Identity Span.** The two furthest-apart people who both do it. (New Balance.)
2. **The Gate.** Explicit callout of who it's for. ("FOR REGULAR RUNNERS," "if you want to get rich in 2026 in the blue-collar industry.")
3. **Self-Recognition.** A thought the viewer has had and never said. ("Are they trail running or just walking really fast?" 2,578.)
4. **Self-Disqualification Pre-empt.** Names their private reason it won't work for them. ("with ZERO credibility.")

**Desire and payoff**
5. **Consequence of the Pain.** Never the pain, the life it causes. ("tired of wearing a hat all the time.")
6. **Two-Number Delta.** Outcome as the distance between two numbers. ("25 at 35," "$35k -> $100K in 12 months," "4K in to 5K.")
7. **Dream Outcome Stated.** Specific, sensory, theirs. (HF6 principle 3.)
8. **Felt-State Testimony.** Unfalsifiable, desirable, first person. ("makes me feel.")
9. **Countable Tools.** Small number of possessable objects, not a topic.

**Authority and proof**
10. **Status Class Import.** An adjective naming a group worth joining. ("elite," "top 1%.")
11. **Credential In The Title.** ("Testing the Vaporfly 4 After Running 2:09.")
12. **Demonstration Instead Of Claim.** Prove the attribute by naming what survives. (Rolls-Royce clock.)
13. **The Boring Process Revealed.** The unglamorous thing nobody explains. (Hopkins/Schlitz, Rice Crisp Graveyard.)
14. **Borrowed Identity.** A named person as subject, only with a real vehicle (P40).

**Tension and jeopardy**
15. **Jeopardy Setup.** A test that can fail. ("the bar that worked in a food lab was about to meet 1,200 metres of climbing," 1,725.)
16. **Premise-Loaded Title.** The whole pitch in the title. (*Snakes on a Plane*.)
17. **Contradiction.** ("I'm Fitter… But I Made a Mistake.")
18. **Consensus Attack.** Requires a real orthodoxy. ("Stop doing zone 2," "Why Most Marathon Runners Train Completely Wrong.")
19. **Diagnosis Reversal.** Negates the viewer's self-diagnosis, replaces it with a specific mechanism. ("You're Not Unfit. You're Underfuelled.") Use sparingly, never vague.
20. **Permissible Transgression.** A sanctioned cheat. ("How to 'Steal' Credibility.")

**Risk removal**
21. **Objection Grave.** The parenthetical buries the biggest reason to scroll. ("without losing quality," "It only takes 3 Hours a Week.")
22. **Deployability Clause.** ("you can use anytime," "even while working a 9-5.")
23. **Time Anchor.** Attaches it to a real slot in the day. ("morning.")

## Part 12 — Construction order

Reversed from how this system has been working. The order is the point.

1. **Name the dream outcome in the viewer's own words.** Not the topic.
   If it can't be said as something they'd say about their own life,
   there is no video yet.
2. **Name the consequence of the pain**, the behaviour or the life the
   problem is actually causing. This is usually the hook, not the pain
   itself.
3. **Choose the vocabulary depth to match the TAM.** Broad video,
   shallow words. Core-audience video, iceberg words pulled from the
   real Reddit research.
4. **Check the awareness stage.** Problem-Aware readers need their
   problem named in their language, not your category name.
5. **Check market sophistication.** In running, assume stage 4-5: lead
   with mechanism or identity, never a bare claim.
6. **Find the single biggest reason they'd scroll past**, and bury it
   in the parenthetical.
7. **Look for two numbers with a gap between them.** Two beat one, one
   beats none.
8. **Choose the shape from the right account's distribution.** Personal:
   how-to-with-gain, scenario-question, self-recognition. ERN: jeopardy,
   test, underdog narrative, never how-to.
9. **Run the film test:** sayable, visualisable, ownable.
10. **Answer the three questions from Part 1.** If question 2 has no
    answer, it isn't finished, however interesting it is.
