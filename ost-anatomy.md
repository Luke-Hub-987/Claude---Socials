# The Anatomy of On-Screen Text

This file exists because the system kept producing on-screen text that
passed every mechanical rule already written down (P21 specificity, P32
concrete tail, P37 keyword) and still landed as "absolutely suck." That
means the rules were describing the surface of what works and missing
the machine underneath. This is the machine.

---

## Part 1 — The job an OST actually does

A viewer gives an on-screen text roughly one second before deciding.
In that second the text has to resolve three questions, and it has to
resolve them **simultaneously**, not in sequence:

1. **"Is this about me?"** (identification)
2. **"What do I get?"** (dream outcome)
3. **"What does it cost me to find out?"** (risk and effort)

Almost every failed OST in this repo's history failed question 2 or 3
while passing question 1. A hook that names a real problem the viewer
has ("2.9kg", "Junk Miles") answers question 1 beautifully and leaves
questions 2 and 3 completely unanswered. The viewer recognises
themselves, feels no promise, and scrolls.

Note what is NOT on this list: "is this interesting?" Interesting is
not a job. Interesting is what a fact is when it has no owner and no
payoff attached. The system has been optimising for interesting.

## Part 2 — Teardown: "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio"

New Balance. Luke quoted it as "models in London and dads in Ohio";
the version usually cited carries "supermodels," which if correct
widens the mechanism below rather than changing it. Worth confirming
before ever quoting it publicly, per P39.

### The structure

`[product, unnamed] + worn by + [Pole A: maximum status, maximum
distance] + and + [Pole B: minimum status, maximum familiarity]`

### What is actually happening

**1. It is a span, not a list.** Two points define a range, and the
reader's mind auto-fills everything between them. A list of five
customer types would feel smaller than this line, because a list is
finite and a span is infinite. The reader does the multiplication.

**2. It resolves the category's core anxiety in one move.** The
central fear in buying clothing is a two-sided trap: *will I look like
I'm trying too hard* and *is this actually still cool*. "Supermodels in
London" answers the second. "Dads in Ohio" answers the first. Any line
that picks one pole solves half the problem. This line solves both, and
solves them *with the same eight words*. That economy is the craft.

**3. "Ohio" is doing work no adjective could.** Not "America." Not
"the Midwest." Not "small towns." Ohio is chosen because it is
maximally specific and minimally glamorous, and because a proper noun
carries a compressed identity that no descriptor can. Swap in "dads in
New York" and the line dies instantly, because the distance between the
poles collapses. **The message lives in the gap between the two nouns,
not in the nouns themselves.**

**4. Forced self-location.** You cannot read the line without silently
answering "which one am I?" That involuntary act of self-placement is
engagement, and it happens before any conscious decision to keep
reading. This is the strongest identification mechanism available in
copy: not describing the reader, but making the reader place
themselves.

**5. The benefit is never stated.** It says who wears it and lets the
reader derive what that means. An amateur writes "comfortable trainers
for everyone." The professional writes the two data points and trusts
the reader to compute "everyone" themselves. A derived conclusion is
held more strongly than a stated one, because the reader believes they
thought of it.

**6. It sounds pre-existing.** Two-beat parallel structure, near-matched
syllable weight, L-alliteration in "London." It scans like a proverb.
Proverb-shaped sentences borrow authority they have not earned, because
the ear associates that rhythm with received wisdom.

### The extractable mechanism: **The Identity Span**

> Name the two furthest-apart people who both do the thing. The gap
> between them is the message.

Running application, unbuilt, for illustration only: "The pace that
works for 2:30 marathoners and 5-hour first-timers." The span does the
work; the tension between the poles creates the curiosity.

## Part 3 — Teardown: "4 elite conversation starters you can use anytime"

### The structure

`[small countable number] + [status class adjective] + [concrete
portable tool, plural] + [deployability clause that kills the main
objection]`

### Word by word

**"4"** does three jobs at once. It caps the time cost (question 3 from
Part 1, answered before the viewer even asks it). It signals curation,
because a specific small number implies the creator filtered a larger
set down. And it sits below the note-taking threshold: 4 things feel
memorable, 10 things feel like homework. Round numbers read as padded;
4 reads as *what was actually left after the cut*.

**"elite"** is the highest-leverage word in the line, and it is doing a
job most people misread. It is not describing quality. It is naming a
**class of person** who uses these, and offering membership. The viewer
is not buying conversation starters, they are buying entry. Note also
what it replaces: a credential. The creator never has to establish why
they know this, because "elite" imports the authority from an implied
group.

**"conversation starters"** is a *tool*, not a *topic*. It is countable,
portable, possessable. You can picture holding one. Compare to "how to
be better at socialising," which is a topic: abstract, unpossessable,
and therefore unmemorable. **Tools beat topics because the viewer can
imagine ownership.**

**"you can use anytime"** is risk rehearsal, and it is aimed at a very
specific unstated objection. The fear with any social advice is "this
only works if you're already confident / at parties / with strangers /
not in my industry." "Anytime" pre-empts every version of that
objection with one word, and it converts the offer from *knowledge* to
*utility*. You do not merely learn them, you deploy them. Second person
puts the viewer in the sentence as the actor.

**What is deliberately absent:** no "how to," no withheld secret, no
curiosity gap at all. This line hides nothing. It works because the
value is concrete enough that curiosity is unnecessary. That is worth
sitting with, because this system has been treating curiosity as
mandatory. It is not. Curiosity is what you use when the value is not
self-evident.

### The extractable mechanism: **Countable Tools + Status Class + Deployability**

## Part 4 — Teardown: "morning mobility routine that makes me feel 25 at 35"

### The structure

`[time-anchored named practice] + that makes me + [felt-state outcome
expressed as a two-number delta]`

### Word by word

**"morning"** anchors the practice to a real slot in the viewer's
actual day. It silently answers "when would I even do this," and it
implies brevity, because mornings are constrained. One word, two
objections dead.

**"mobility routine"** is the keyword, and note carefully what kind of
keyword it is: **plain language for something the viewer already
half-wants.** It is not a coined insider term. This is the single most
important correction to this system's recent behaviour, which has been
inventing or borrowing exotic keywords ("Cadence Lock") on the belief
that novelty of naming is what P37 asks for. It is not. The keyword's
job is to name a *possessable thing*, and the most powerful version of
that is usually a phrase the viewer already recognises and wants.

**"that makes me feel"** is testimony, not theory, and it is present
tense and ongoing. It also makes a claim about a **felt state**, which
is strategically brilliant: felt states are unfalsifiable and therefore
unchallengeable, while simultaneously being what the viewer actually
wants. "Will make you faster" invites argument. "Makes me feel" cannot
be argued with.

**"25 at 35"** is the engine of the entire line.

- Two numbers, ten years apart, in four syllables.
- The viewer performs the subtraction involuntarily. A computed benefit
  lands harder than a stated one, same principle as the New Balance
  span.
- It expresses the dream outcome as a **numeric delta of felt age**,
  which converts an abstract desire (feeling good) into something that
  reads as evidence. Numbers read as proof even when the underlying
  claim is subjective.
- It is self-targeting. A 35-year-old sees themselves exactly. A
  45-year-old extrapolates and wants it more.
- The digits reverse: 25 / 35. Chiasmic, near-rhyming, memorable. It
  sounds like a formula, which again borrows authority.

**The creator is the proof, not the hero.** "makes *me* feel" positions
him as the tested case, with the implicit offer being "do what I did,
get what I got." That is exactly StoryBrand's guide role (P30): the
creator's experience exists to de-risk the viewer's attempt, not to be
admired.

### The extractable mechanism: **Named Practice + Felt-State Outcome as a Two-Number Delta**

## Part 5 — What Luke's own account data actually proves

This is the most valuable evidence in the repo and it has been
under-read. P32 looked at the winners and concluded "the tail must be
concrete." That is true but incomplete, and the incompleteness is why
output kept failing. Reading the winners *and the losers* together
gives a sharper rule.

### Winners, with their tails (real view counts, `voice.md`)

| OST | Views | What the tail names |
|---|---|---|
| "Are they trail running... or just walking really fast?" | 2,578 | no tail, self-recognition joke |
| "Q: If it's hot on race day, should I pour water over myself?" | 2,505 | no tail, real decision moment |
| "How to actually use strides (To get you a PB)" | 2,266 | **viewer's gain** |
| "How to leave on time (Without killing your career)" | 2,260 | **viewer's objection killed** |
| "How do I distinguish 'good' race pain from injury? (and you can't tell the difference)" | 1,891 | **viewer's stakes** |
| "3 reasons I run on holiday (the 3rd is the one nobody admits)" | 1,830 | **viewer's curiosity payoff** |
| "How to build a sauna into your easy run *(to make racing easier)" | 1,770 | **viewer's gain** |
| "How to FULLY finish every workout (Far stronger than everyone else)" | 1,759 | **viewer's gain, comparative** |
| "How to become so f**kin interesting - everyone will want to work with you" | 1,662 | **viewer's gain, social** |
| "Q: When does running 20K feel 'normal'? (How to get there)" | 1,610 | **viewer's path** |
| "How to stop letting your head end your runs early *(4K in to 5K)" | 1,539 | **viewer's gain, quantified** |

### Losers, with their tails

| OST | Views | What the tail names |
|---|---|---|
| "Q: How to know if you 'actually' raced? (When most take part)" | 133 | an abstract judgement |
| "How to run when you 'think' everyone is watching (*Spotlight effect)" | 313 | **the mechanism's jargon name** |
| "One missed session out of 1,200 hours (but you lie awake like it's all over)" | 462 | **a feeling** |
| "The 'real' problem with running everything easy (+what I do now)" | 518 | **the creator's behaviour** |
| "How I Went From Run/Walk To 3K Straight (1 Lamppost At A Time)" | 561 | **the method's name** |
| "Feeling slow asf at Parkrun" | 606 | nothing |
| "When you 'actually' need a gel (*To absolutely smash your race)" | 631 | viewer's gain, but hyped and vague |
| "How to log off without guilt" | 679 | gain, but no specificity |

### The rule this produces

> **The tail must name what the VIEWER walks away with. Not the
> mechanism, not the method's name, not the creator's process, not a
> mood.**

Every tail above 1,500 names the viewer's gain, stakes, objection, or
path. Every tail below 700 names a mechanism, a method, a feeling, or
the creator. "1 Lamppost At A Time" is concrete, specific, real, and
charming, and it did 561, because concreteness was never the variable.
**Ownership of the payoff was.**

### Two more findings from the same data

**"How to" beats "How I."** "How to actually use strides" did 2,266.
"How I Went From Run/Walk To 3K Straight" did 561. Same account, same
audience, same era. The second word of the headline decides whose story
it is, and the viewer only stays for their own.

**The honest counter-example.** "When you 'actually' need a gel (*To
absolutely smash your race)" has a viewer-gain tail and did 631. The
rule is directional, not absolute. Best available reading: the tail is
a hype phrase ("absolutely smash") rather than a specific outcome, and
the topic is narrow. Held as a live caveat rather than explained away,
per P36.

**The two non-how-to winners do a different job entirely.** "Are they
trail running or just walking really fast?" (2,578) and "What's it
actually like to Climb Benbulben" (148,000) are not teaching anything.
The first is self-recognition, an in-group joke the viewer has thought
but never said. The second is vicarious experience. Neither promises a
gain, because entertainment and vicarious experience *are* the gain.
These are legitimate, and they are a separate category with separate
rules. Do not judge them by the tail rule.

## Part 6 — What the 80-example swipe file proves

Counted across the real corpus in
`research/briar-cochran-hook-titles-and-swipefile.txt`:

- **"How to [outcome] ([objection or constraint])" is the dominant
  shape**, roughly 35% of Briar's own posted OSTs. Examples: "How to
  post 3x/day (without losing quality)," "How to be more natural on
  camera (3 practical tips)," "How to make content with ZERO
  credibility."
- **Named proprietary concepts are a minority**, roughly 15%: "The
  Accordion Method," "Hookmaxxing," "The 'Stairs Steps of Content'
  Theory," "Super Hooks."
- Numbered lists, before/after journeys, and tier lists make up most of
  the rest.

**This is the correction.** This system's last batch of 10 was 6 named
concepts, 2 bare numbers, 1 how-to, 1 numbered list. That inverts the
distribution of the corpus it was supposedly learning from, and it
inverts Luke's own winners, which are dominated by how-to-with-gain and
scenario-questions. P37 was read as "always coin a concept." The real
corpus says the keyword is usually just the plain name of the thing,
sitting inside a how-to.

### Other machinery visible in the corpus

- **Objection killed in the parenthetical**: "(without losing
  quality)," "(It only takes 3 Hours a Week)," "(Even while working a
  9-5)," "(when most don't)." The parenthetical is where the reason not
  to watch goes to die.
- **Two numbers, one line**: "How To Become Noticeably More Athletic in
  90 Days (It only takes 3 Hours a Week)" states time-to-result AND
  price. "I scaled my coffee cart from $35k -> $100K in 12 months"
  states before, after, and duration.
- **The anti-brag**: "I made only $7k the first year with my coffee
  cart. But this one FRAMEWORK kept me going." Lowballing the number
  buys trust that a big number would spend.
- **One-word urgency**: "You're Not Behind (yet)." The "(yet)"
  reintroduces the exact fear the headline just soothed. Six words,
  two emotional states.
- **Comprehension permission**: "Explaining PLYOMETRICS but YOU are NOT
  Smarter than a 5th Grader." Kills the real fear with technical
  content, which is not disinterest but anticipated confusion.
- **Permissible transgression**: "How to 'Steal' Credibility as a
  Creator." Scare quotes signal the transgression is sanctioned.
  Transgression plus permission is one of the strongest curiosity
  compounds available.
- **Self-disqualification pre-empted**: "How to make content with ZERO
  credibility." Names the viewer's private reason for not starting.
- **Consensus attack**: "Stop doing zone 2." Three words. Only works
  when an orthodoxy exists in that audience to push against.

## Part 7 — The mechanism library

Named, so they can be chosen deliberately rather than stumbled into.

1. **Identity Span.** Two furthest-apart people who both do it. (New
   Balance.)
2. **Two-Number Delta.** Outcome expressed as the distance between two
   numbers. ("25 at 35," "$35k -> $100k in 12 months," "4K in to 5K.")
3. **Countable Tools.** A small number of possessable objects, not a
   topic. ("4 elite conversation starters.")
4. **Status Class Import.** An adjective that names a group worth
   joining, standing in for a credential. ("elite," "top 1%.")
5. **Deployability Clause.** Kills the "but not in my situation"
   objection. ("you can use anytime," "even while working a 9-5.")
6. **Objection Grave.** The parenthetical holds the single biggest
   reason not to watch, and buries it. ("without losing quality.")
7. **Time Anchor.** Attaches the practice to a real slot in the day.
   ("morning.")
8. **Felt-State Testimony.** An unfalsifiable, highly desirable
   subjective outcome, first person. ("makes me feel.")
9. **Self-Recognition.** States a thought the viewer has had and never
   said aloud. ("Are they trail running or just walking really fast?")
10. **Vicarious Experience.** Promises a feeling or a place, not a
    lesson. ("What's it actually like to Climb Benbulben.")
11. **Real Decision Moment.** A specific fork the viewer will actually
    face. ("If it's hot on race day, should I pour water over myself?")
12. **Comprehension Permission.** Removes the fear of not
    understanding. ("but YOU are NOT Smarter than a 5th Grader.")
13. **Permissible Transgression.** A sanctioned cheat. ("How to 'Steal'
    Credibility.")
14. **Self-Disqualification Pre-empt.** Names the viewer's private
    reason they think this won't work for them. ("with ZERO
    credibility.")
15. **Consensus Attack.** Contradicts a known orthodoxy in that
    audience. ("Stop doing zone 2.")
16. **Anti-Brag.** A deliberately unimpressive real number, used to buy
    trust before the mechanism.
17. **One-Word Urgency.** A single word that reopens the fear.
    ("(yet).")

## Part 8 — Construction method

Order matters. This is the sequence that produces the examples above,
run in reverse from how the system has been working.

1. **Name the dream outcome in the viewer's own words first.** Not the
   topic. The outcome. If it cannot be written as something the viewer
   would say out loud about their own life ("I want to stop dying at
   mile 20," "I want to feel 25 again"), there is no video yet.
2. **Name the possessable thing that delivers it.** A routine, a test,
   a rule, a set of tools, a decision. This is the keyword (P37), and
   it should usually be plain language, not a coined term.
3. **Find the single biggest reason the viewer would scroll past.** Not
   watch, scroll. That reason goes in the parenthetical, dead.
4. **Look for a number that can carry the outcome.** Two numbers with a
   gap between them beat one number. One real number beats none.
5. **Choose the shape from the corpus distribution, not from habit.**
   Roughly: how-to-with-gain as the default, scenario-question and
   self-recognition as the strong alternates, named concept as the
   minority option reserved for when the thing genuinely needs a name.
6. **Read it back and answer the three questions from Part 1.** If
   question 2 has no answer, it is not finished, no matter how
   interesting it is.

## Part 9 — The kill list

An OST is not finished if any of these are true.

- The tail names a mechanism, a method's name, a feeling, or the
  creator, rather than the viewer's payoff.
- The headline opens "How I" where "How to" was available.
- It states an interesting fact and promises nothing. (The "2.9kg"
  failure.)
- The keyword is a coined term the viewer has never heard and has no
  reason to want. (The "Cadence Lock" failure.)
- It borrows another person's achievement with no vehicle connecting
  the creator to it. (The "Badwater" failure, P40.)
- The parenthetical contains a hype word ("absolutely smash,"
  "insane," "game-changing") where a specific outcome belongs.
- It requires the viewer to already care about the topic before the
  benefit appears.
- It could have been written by someone who had not done the research.

## What this file changes

P21, P29, P30, P32, P37 and HF6 are all still correct as far as they
go. This file is what sits underneath them: the outcome comes first and
the keyword serves it, the parenthetical is a grave for objections
rather than a footnote for mechanisms, and the shape should be drawn
from the real corpus distribution instead of defaulting to whichever
device was most recently praised.
