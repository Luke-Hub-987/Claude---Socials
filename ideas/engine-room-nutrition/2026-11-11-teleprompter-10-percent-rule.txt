Spring marathon on the 10% rule? It misses the one run that gets you hurt, because it watches your week.

That run puts you on the sofa in March, but spot it and you'll start in April with nothing sore.

We call it the 30-day check, so save this.

The 10% rule has been properly tested once, on 532 new runners. About 1 in 5 got hurt, the same as a normal plan, so the rule bought them nothing. But a newer study of 5,200 runners' watch data found what came before injuries. The weekly jump wasn't linked, but one run more than a tenth past your 30-day longest was. So stop checking your week and start checking one run.

Say your longest recent run was 16K, on a 45K week. On Sunday you go 20K, so your week rises under 10% and the rule waves it through. But that 20K is a quarter longer than anything your legs have done in 30 days. Runs like that came with a 64% higher injury rate in the study. Your lungs adapt in a few weeks, but tendons take far longer, so they're still built for 16K. Before your next long run, open your watch and find your longest from the last 30 days.

That one run decides how far Sunday is allowed to go.

If your longest was 16K, your next long run stops at 17.6K. Run that, and the next can reach 19.4K, so you still climb to your plan's longest. The limit for every longest run from 10K to 28K is in the caption. Plans for Barcelona and Rome start on 23 November, when your long runs start climbing. Check before every one, because the 10% rule never will.
