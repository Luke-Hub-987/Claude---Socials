## Caption
Spring marathon on the 10% rule? It checks your week, but the run that gets runners hurt is the one long run that goes past anything from the last 30 days.

The rule's one trial (Buist 2008, 532 new runners) found injuries in 20.8% on a 13-week plan built on it and 20.3% on a standard 8-week plan: no difference. A 2025 study of 5,205 runners' Garmin data over 18 months (Frandsen, BJSM) found no link between the week-to-week jump and injury. It compared every run with the longest in the previous 30 days: runs 10% to 30% longer came with a 64% higher injury rate, and runs more than double came with a 128% higher rate. It's an observational study, so it shows a link, not a cause.

The 30-day check: before every long run, find your longest run in the last 30 days. The study's injury rate started climbing a tenth past it, so that's your limit. Longest 10K, next one up to 11K. Longest 14K, up to 15.4K. Longest 16K, up to 17.6K. Longest 18K, up to 19.8K. Longest 20K, up to 22K. Longest 24K, up to 26.4K. Longest 28K, up to 30.8K. Each time you go further, that run becomes the new longest, so from 16K you climb 17.6K, 19.4K, 21.3K, 23.4K, 25.8K, 28.3K and 31.2K, one long run at a time, up to your plan's longest.

The worked example: a 16K longest on a 45K week. A 20K Sunday puts the week up 4K, under 10%, so the weekly rule allows it, but it's 25% past your longest, the band that came with the 64% higher rate. Under the 30-day check, that Sunday is 17.6K.

Why: your heart and lungs catch up in a few weeks, but tendons take much longer to adapt to load, so they're only ready for what you've done lately.

When your 16 weeks start: Barcelona and Rome on 23 November, Manchester on 28 December, London on 4 January.

Want a spring plan built on it? Email us your race and goal time through the link in the bio and we'll send it free.

## Reply ready for "isn't that just 10% again?"
