## Aha #1 (first 2-3 seconds, before any credibility beat)

A real first-time marathoner averaged 180bpm for 5 hours straight and
asked if their heart was broken. Here's the non-obvious part: an
average heart rate on its own can't tell you if something's wrong,
because a number alone doesn't say how hard that actually was for you.
That same poster could still hold a normal conversation at 180bpm and
only got winded near 200. The number scared them. The number wasn't the
story.

## Aha #2 (body block 2, the specific mechanism, named-concept payoff)

Heart rate climbs on its own the longer you're out there, even at the
same effort and pace, because of heat, dehydration, and hours on your
feet. It's called cardiac drift, and it's normal. A training zone you
hit in a clean 45-minute session (say, 150bpm) was never going to hold
at hour 4 of a marathon. A higher number late in a race is usually your
heart doing exactly what hours of drift does to everyone, not your
heart in trouble.

## Payoff (body block 3, the practical close)

The test that actually matters isn't the average on your watch after
the fact, it's two things in the moment: can you still talk, and is the
number climbing sharply and staying dangerously high, or drifting the
way hours on your feet always makes it drift. Check the trend, not a
single scary peak.

## Feeling

Relief, not alarm — a scary number pulled out of context isn't a
diagnosis, and the talk test in the moment beats a static number on a
screen after the fact.

## CTA

Soft engagement: ask what the highest heart rate they've ever seen on a
race was, and whether they panicked when they saw it.
