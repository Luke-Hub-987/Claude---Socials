# Craft Reference — The Analytical Athlete (LinkedIn)

**This project is fully self-contained.** Everything the
`/ideate-analytical-athlete` and `/onboard-analytical-athlete` skills
need lives under `linkedin-analytical-athlete/`. Per Luke's direct
instruction (2026-09-07): LinkedIn is a completely separate project
from the IG/TikTok/YouTube system in the rest of this repo — what
works on one platform doesn't necessarily work on another, so this
file holds its own copy of every rule and craft principle used here,
rather than pointing back at `principles.md`, `hook-frameworks.md`,
`ost-*.md`, or `voice-rules.md` at the repo root. Those files are for
the personal/ERN video brands only — don't read or edit them for
LinkedIn work, and don't edit this file's source-of-truth back into
them either.

Some of the concepts below were originally developed for that other
system and adapted here as a one-time copy (attributed inline where
relevant). From this point on, this file is the independent source of
truth for LinkedIn — if a rule needs to change for LinkedIn, change it
here, not in the root-level files, and vice versa.

## Universal style rules

- No em-dashes anywhere, in hooks, body text, or CTAs. Use a period,
  comma, or parentheses instead.
- No corporate/LinkedIn-speak filler: "in today's world," "let's dive
  in," "unlock your potential," "game-changer."
- No hedging language in hooks: "I think," "maybe," "just my opinion."
  State the position.
- No hashtag walls. If hashtags are used, 3-5 max, relevant only.
- Never fabricate a research claim, statistic, or study. If a claim
  can't be traced to something real (a source inside this project, or
  something Luke has explicitly confirmed, or a citation genuinely
  verified via search and clearly flagged as such), don't state it as
  fact — mark it "NEEDS REAL CITATION" or "NEEDS VERIFICATION" instead.

## No AI-slop language, absolute, no carve-outs

The single most important rule in this file. Checked against
[Wikipedia: Signs of AI Writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
(maintained by WikiProject AI Cleanup) every time a hook is written,
not just once.

- **Negative parallelism / tailing negation, banned outright, no
  exceptions:** "not just X, but Y," "it's not X, it's Y," "X isn't Y,
  it's Z," "X rather than Y" as the entire mechanism of a line. This is
  the single most-flagged pattern and the one most likely to slip in
  unnoticed, including in a "corrected" form where the twist is just
  moved one clause later — it's still the same pattern and still
  banned. **Fix pattern: deliver the corrected-diagnosis mechanism as a
  single positive statement instead of a reversal** — "The pasta
  dinner the night before is 48 hours too late," not "It's not the
  pasta, it's the timing."
- **Avoidance of plain "is/are":** don't reach for "serves as," "stands
  as," "marks," "functions as," "represents" where "is" would do.
- **Marketing verbs standing in for "has":** "features," "boasts,"
  "offers," "maintains" used unnaturally.
- **Overused AI vocabulary:** "delve," "intricate/intricacies,"
  "meticulous," "tapestry," "underscore," "garner," "bolstered," "align
  with," "enhance," "fostering," "showcasing," "emphasizing," "deep
  dive," "additionally" as a transition crutch.
- **Promotional/inflated tone:** "boasts," "vibrant," "profound,"
  "groundbreaking," "renowned," "game-changing," "pivotal moment,"
  "testament to," "stands as a reminder."
- **Rule-of-three padding:** three parallel items listed purely for
  rhythm, not because there are actually three things worth saying. If
  a real count is 2, 4, or 5, use that.
- **Present-participle padding:** trailing "-ing" clauses added for
  vague superficial analysis ("highlighting their significance").
- **Vague attribution:** "industry reports show," "experts argue,"
  "observers have noted" without a real, named, checkable source.
- **Excessive boldface, title-case abuse, emoji-as-formatting.**
- **The "challenges" formula:** "Despite its [positive], [subject]
  faces challenges..." as a rigid, formulaic structure.

## The LinkedIn hook is on-screen text: the same craft applies

A headline's whole job is "words in order to make people read, want,
and feel something" — exactly what on-screen text does for video.
Before finalizing any hook, run it through:

**Five hook principles** (adapted from on-screen-text craft):
1. **Curiosity** — allude to something revealed later.
2. **Pain point** — name the reader's specific fear/frustration
   directly, don't soften it.
3. **Dream outcome** — state a specific (never vague, never generic)
   thing the reader actually wants.
4. **Tangibility** — make the outcome picturable, not abstract.
5. **Whisper technique** — a parenthetical or trailing clause that
   builds curiosity for later, or does risk rehearsal (pre-empts the
   #1 reason someone would scroll past).

**CCN — Core, Casual, New.** Every hook should work for three layers
at once:
- **Core** — people who already take this topic seriously.
- **Casual** — people who dip in and out, interested but not deep.
- **New** — people outside the topic entirely who'd still engage if
  the framing is specific enough.
The mechanism that hits all three at once is **specific curiosity**,
not maximum stakes. Tag every idea's CCN read explicitly: name who's
Core, who's Casual, who's New, and what makes each of them stay.

**The newspaper-headline bar: specificity beats reversal.** A hook has
to pass the test a real newspaper headline would: would a stranger
with zero context want to read this because of a concrete, specific
claim, not because a withheld answer is being teased? "There are 3
fuelling mistakes that only show up after kilometre 30" clears this
bar. "The secret to fuelling a long run isn't what you think" fails
it — no number, no concrete claim, nothing a reader couldn't have
predicted the shape of.

**One example is a device to add to the rotation, never a template to
stamp.** When a single hook works well, extract the structural device
it demonstrates and add it to the menu below — don't let it dominate
the next batch. No single device should account for more than roughly
a quarter of any given batch of ideas. Name each idea's device
explicitly and check the batch's distribution before finalizing.

**Real, proven hook shapes** (a starting menu, not an exhaustive list):
- **Scenario-question** — a real, specific first-person moment stated
  as a question, no mechanism explained yet ("Have you ever had to
  stop mid-run to decide if that gel is coming back up?").
- **Numbered taxonomy** — a flat, real, specific count, not rounded
  for rhythm ("There are 3 fuelling mistakes that only show up after
  kilometre 30").
- **Ladder/progression** — ranked levels the reader places themselves
  on, the loop only closes at the top level.
- **Named-tier/borrowed-authority** — a real, checkable tier or person
  ("Sub-2:30 marathoners start taking on carbs at kilometre 5").
- **Confession/secret** — a behavior "nobody admits" but almost
  everyone does.
- **Effort-to-outcome gap** — a small, specific input tied to a large,
  specific consequence.
- **Money** — cost/value framed literally (what gets spent vs. what
  gets prevented).
- **Research-citation** — a real, named, checkable study or number.
- **First-person admission** — a true, specific, personal detail,
  never invented for effect.

**One nameable keyword.** Every idea should compress to one thing the
reader can silently ask "do I already know about that" and decide to
stay or scroll based on — plain language for something they already
half-want (a training concept, a real number, a named moment), not a
coined insider term.

## Power-word bank

Words are not decoration — each does one structural job. **One power
word per line, in one slot.** Two starts sounding written. A power
word cannot rescue a weak underlying structure — if the specificity
isn't already there, the adjective just makes the flatness louder.
Rotate the *job*, not just the word — don't let the same word appear
across every idea in a batch.

| Job | Words |
|---|---|
| Price it as costly | uncomfortable, brutal, unglamorous, humbling, boring, awkward, thankless |
| Make it transgressive | ruthless, illegal, banned, unhinged, cheating, unfair, dangerously |
| Make it secret | quietly, secretly, nobody admits, never says out loud, off camera, unspoken |
| Make it counterintuitive | backwards, wrong way round, insane, absurd, makes no sense |
| Borrow authority | elite, sub-2:30, world-class, coach, Olympic, championship |
| Make it irreversible | for good, permanently, never again, ends your race, gone |
| Set the bomb (name the moment) | until mile 30, at 30k, at 6am, with 2 miles to go, at 34k |
| State the consequence | wrecks, costs you, ends, blows up, comes back up, hand back |

## Post structure (direct-response model)

1. **Opening line = the whole hook**, a single sharp claim or
   contrast, never a scene-setter.
2. **A short, plain-language mechanism or citation** — a real number,
   a named study, a physiological fact, never vague.
3. **Reframe onto the reader's own life**, second person, present
   tense.
4. **Close with a direct, named CTA** to the newsletter, with a
   current subscriber count as social proof and a single link — never
   vague, never omitted.
